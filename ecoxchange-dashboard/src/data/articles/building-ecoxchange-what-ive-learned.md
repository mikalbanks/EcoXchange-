When I started EcoXchange, I thought the hard part would be the technology. It
turned out the technology was the *clear* part. The hard part was discipline —
deciding what we would refuse to do.

## The founding premise

Accredited investors have no direct, production-verified way to own individual
solar projects. Every available vehicle — pooled funds, REITs, yieldcos,
crowdfunding — delivers blended, fund-level performance. I wanted the opposite:
one security, one project, one auditable production trail.

## Three things I have learned

**1. Verification is a product, not a feature.** Early on I treated the
reconciliation engine as plumbing. It is actually the whole point. The moment you
pay distributions on *verified* rather than *projected* production, you are
selling trust, and trust has to be engineered as carefully as any feature.

**2. Regulation is a design constraint, not an afterthought.** Building under Reg D
506(c) shaped the architecture from day one — what we capture at onboarding, how
we document suitability, what counsel must review before anything publishes. The
teams that bolt compliance on later pay for it twice.

**3. Boring infrastructure beats clever infrastructure.** A satellite model that
is right 99% of the time and easy to audit is worth more than an exotic one that
is right 99.5% of the time and impossible to explain. Investors are trusting us
with capital; legibility is a feature.

> The principles that guide EcoXchange are simple: verify before you pay, document
> before you publish, and never show a number you cannot defend.

## What is next

We are deepening the verification engine, expanding the developer pipeline, and
building the investor experience this journal is part of. I will keep writing here
as we go — including the mistakes. Transparency is not a marketing line for a
securities platform; it is the job.

— Mikal
